# IntraWeb Technologies — Proof Density Doctrine & Implementation Compression Rules
**Phase:** Pre-copywriting governance. Final constraint layer before execution.**  
**Version:** 3.0 — May 2026  
**Authority:** Governs all copywriting, visual decisions, and implementation choices across the site.  
**Use:** Apply this document as a QA filter at the end of every copywriting session and before any section goes to implementation.

---

## How to Use This Document

This document does not describe what the site should say. It describes the constraints under which everything is written and built. It is enforcement doctrine, not creative direction.

Apply it in two modes:
- **During writing:** Use compression rules and density doctrine as active constraints on every section.
- **After writing:** Use the Anti-Drift Enforcement Layer (Part 5) as a QA pass on completed sections.

---

## Part 1: Proof Density Doctrine

### Governing Principle

The site should feel evidence-backed, not evidence-saturated. Proof earns trust when it is specific, contextual, and placed at the moment the visitor needs it. Proof placed before the visitor has a question it answers is decoration. Proof placed after the visitor has already converted is waste.

### The Proof Escalation Model

Proof is not evenly distributed across the site. It escalates as the visitor moves from initial orientation to engagement readiness. Each page in the site has a designated proof role. No page should exceed its role.

**Proof role by page:**

| Page | Proof Role | Proof Format | Maximum Proof Elements |
|------|-----------|--------------|----------------------|
| Homepage | Signal | Proof card summaries, one operational stat | 3 cards + 1 stat bar |
| Services | Credibility anchor | Inline scope examples, 1 outcome reference | 2-3 inline references |
| About | Practitioner proof | Workflow count, origin specificity | 1 stat + origin narrative |
| Work / Operational Records | Full evidence | Complete records in standard format | No ceiling — this is the primary proof container |
| Diagnostic | Process credibility | Deliverable specifics, outcome reference | 1 outcome reference |
| Individual Record | Deep evidence | Full seven-section format | Per record format rules |

**The escalation rule:** A visitor who has read the homepage should want more evidence. The Work page delivers it. A visitor who has read the Work page should want to understand the engagement. The Diagnostic page delivers it. Proof escalates — it does not repeat at equal density across pages.

### Homepage Proof Density Rules

- Maximum three proof cards. Not four. Not five.
- Each card carries one impact metric. Not a list of metrics — one.
- One operational stat bar (total workflows, cycle time reference, or similar). One location on the page. Not repeated.
- No testimonials on the homepage. The proof cards are the evidence layer. Quotes are not evidence — they are sentiment. Sentiment is not the register of this site.
- The proof cards link to the full Operational Records, not to a generic "learn more" destination.
- Homepage proof density ceiling: a visitor should be able to absorb all homepage proof in under 90 seconds of active reading. If a full reading takes longer, the homepage is over-proofed.

### Services Page Proof Rules

- The Services page does not carry primary proof responsibility. It carries scope credibility.
- Inline proof on Services is limited to one concrete example per engagement type. Not a case study summary — a single operational sentence illustrating what the engagement produces.
  - Acceptable: "A professional services company with four systems connected reduced their onboarding cycle from 18 steps to 7."
  - Not acceptable: A proof card reproduced within the Services page.
- No impact metrics on Services beyond scope illustrations. Services describes what gets built. Work/Operational Records proves it worked.
- The Services page's primary conversion function is to qualify, not to convince. Proof belongs on Work. Services points to Work for visitors who need evidence before scope clarity.

### About Page Proof Rules

- The About page carries practitioner proof, not outcome proof.
- One operational stat on About. The 62 workflows figure is the right choice — it is a practitioner signal, not a marketing claim.
- The origin narrative is proof by specificity. It does not carry metrics. Attempting to add metrics to the origin narrative converts it from a trust document into a pitch document.
- No proof card summaries on About. The page's evidence function is different: it proves the operator's depth, not the engagement's outcomes.
- The comparison table (current site: "Why the Approach is Different") is the methodological proof layer. It stays. It is not a proof element to be supplemented — it is complete as a structural comparison.

### Operational Records Proof Rules

- This page has no proof density ceiling — it is the primary proof container for the site. All outcome evidence eventually lives here.
- Each individual record follows the seven-section format exactly — no abbreviated versions on the list page.
- The list/grid page carries only the metadata and a one-sentence operational summary per record. The full evidence is inside the record.
- Records are not summarized elsewhere on the site beyond the homepage cards. A homepage card that summarizes a record should link directly to that record — not to the Work index page.
- The Operational Records index is not a marketing page. It does not have a hero section, a tagline, or a philosophy paragraph. It has a label, a filter bar, and a record grid.

### CTA Proof Rules

- No new proof is introduced in a CTA section. CTAs close on trust already established, not on new evidence.
- A CTA may reference one previously-established proof element as an anchor — "Start with the same Diagnostic that reduced [company type]'s reporting cycle from 12 days to 2" — but this is a callback, not new proof.
- CTAs that introduce new statistics or claims undermine the closing function. The visitor's attention should be on the action, not on evaluating new information.

### Repetition Fatigue Rules

- A specific metric (e.g., "94% faster cycle time") appears in one location on the site — the proof card or the Operational Record it belongs to. It does not migrate to other sections as supporting evidence.
- A pain point named in the Friction Grid does not need to be re-named in the proof section, the services section, or the CTA. Name it once in the recognition layer. Let the proof resolve it without restating it.
- The 62 workflows stat appears once on the homepage (in the stat bar) and once on the About page (in the operator context). It does not appear on Services, Diagnostic, or individual record pages.
- If a visitor reads the homepage and then the About page sequentially, they should encounter no identical sentences. Parallel meaning is acceptable. Identical copy is not.

### Trust Compounding Model

Trust compounds across pages when each page adds a new dimension to the same core claim — it does not compound by repeating the claim at higher volume.

Homepage → "They remove operational friction" (practical claim)  
Work page → "They have documented removals" (evidence)  
About page → "The operator has seen this from the inside and built the right approach" (human grounding)  
Diagnostic page → "I know what the first step looks like" (commitment confidence)

Each page adds a new dimension. None restates the prior page's case.

## Part 2: Compression & Cognitive Load Governance

### Governing Principle

Every sentence that does not advance the visitor's state (Clarity → Recognition → Confidence → Imagination → Action) is a load imposition with no return. The compression rules below are not stylistic preferences — they are cognitive load management constraints.

### Paragraph Length Rules

- **Body paragraphs:** Maximum 3 lines at rendered page width (approximately 50-65 characters per line at typical viewport). A 4-line paragraph is permitted only when it contains a specific operational example that cannot be shortened without losing the specificity.
- **Section-opening paragraphs:** Maximum 2 lines. The first paragraph of any section is a scan target — it tells the visitor whether to read the section. If the first paragraph requires 4 lines to set up the section, the section is framed wrong.
- **Proof card text:** Maximum 15 words per bullet in "What Was Breaking." Impact stat stands alone — no sentence wraps the number.
- **Process step descriptions:** Maximum 2 sentences per step. One sentence: what IntraWeb does. One sentence: what the visitor gets or stops doing.
- **CTA sections:** Maximum 3 lines of body copy before the button. If the CTA requires more than 3 lines of context, the prior sections have not done their work.

### Section Density Rules

**High density sections** (contain multiple data points, lists, or comparison structures):
- Proof cards
- Operational Records "What Was Breaking" and "What Changed" sections
- Services engagement type descriptions
- The comparison table on About

**Medium density sections** (contain 2-3 substantive points):
- Hero secondary text and CTA pair
- Process step descriptions
- Fit indicators
- Diagnostic deliverables list

**Low density sections** (single focused point, generous whitespace):
- Hero primary statement
- Section transition lines
- CTA closing statement
- Ongoing Operational State in records

**Adjacency rule:** No two high-density sections may be adjacent. High density must be followed by medium or low before the next high-density section. This is not a visual preference — it is cognitive recovery management.

### Section Stacking Rules

- Maximum 7 distinct sections on any single page (homepage may reach 7; other pages should target 5-6).
- No section may introduce more than one new abstraction. If a section introduces the concept of "operational infrastructure," it cannot also introduce "compounding change" in the same section. One new concept per section.
- Every conceptual statement must resolve into an operational example within 3 sentences. "We diagnose before we scope" must be followed within three sentences by a description of what that diagnosis involves or produces. Conceptual statements that float without operational grounding are manifesto material.
- A section that introduces a concept and then immediately introduces another concept without a grounding example creates a "concept stack" — two or more abstractions layered without resolution. Concept stacks are the primary cause of "smart but exhausting" site experience.

### Sentence Complexity Rules

- **Maximum clause depth:** Two clauses per sentence. A sentence with three or more subordinate clauses requires the visitor to hold its structure in working memory while parsing meaning. If a sentence requires re-reading to understand, it is too complex.
- **Subject positioning:** The operational subject of a sentence should appear in the first five words. "The manual assembly of weekly reports by the operations team" delays the subject. "The operations team assembled reports manually" leads with the subject.
- **Passive voice:** Permitted only in two contexts: (1) describing a system's action ("The workflow triggers when...") and (2) the Ongoing Operational State section of records ("The report is generated automatically..."). Everywhere else, active voice is required. Passive constructions in proof sections hide the operator (IntraWeb) and the action (what was built), which undermines trust.
- **Abstraction chains:** An abstraction chain is a sentence that uses three or more abstract nouns in sequence ("operational clarity, systems alignment, and infrastructure visibility"). Maximum two abstract nouns per sentence. If a third is needed, convert one of the first two to a concrete example.

### Scan Rhythm Rules

The site must be scannable at three levels:
1. **Section label scan:** A visitor reading only section labels should understand the page's full argument.
2. **First-line scan:** A visitor reading only the first line of each paragraph should understand the section's point.
3. **Full read:** A visitor who reads every word should encounter no contradiction with what the scan implied.

If a section only works as a full read — if the first line is a setup that doesn't communicate the section's value — the first line needs to be rewritten to front-load the point.

### Whitespace Expectations

- Whitespace is not a design preference. It is the visual signal that a cognitive unit has ended and the visitor may process before continuing.
- After every high-density section, a visible pause (increased vertical whitespace, a low-density transition element, or a section break) is required.
- Proof cards require whitespace between cards on desktop — they are distinct units, not a continuous stream.
- The CTA section requires the most whitespace on the page — it should feel like the visitor has arrived somewhere, not that they have run out of content.

## Part 3: Visual Realism Governance

### Governing Principle

Every visual either proves operational reality or it does not belong on the page. Operational visuals earn trust by showing evidence of real systems. Aesthetic visuals impress for a moment and erode trust when the visitor realizes they communicate nothing specific.

### Workflow Screenshot Standards

**Acceptable workflow screenshots:**
- Real n8n canvas screenshots, anonymized if needed (client names removed, generic node labels replaced with operational descriptions)
- Minimum resolution: legible at 1200px wide. A workflow screenshot that requires zooming to read is not useful.
- Canvas scope: show enough of the workflow to communicate the logic — typically one complete flow path. Do not show only a fragment with no entry or exit point visible.
- Background: use the actual tool's interface. Do not recolor, reframe, or stylize the screenshot.

**Annotation rules for workflow screenshots:**
- Annotations are permitted and encouraged when they add operational context
- Maximum 3 annotations per screenshot — more creates visual noise that the visitor must manage
- Annotation format: short callout label, maximum 5 words, pointing to a specific node or connection
- Annotations must name what the step does, not what tool it uses. "Scores lead 0-100" not "Claude API call"
- Annotation color: single color, high contrast to the canvas background. Not multiple colors — multiple annotation colors create hierarchy confusion.

### Caption Structure

Every visual element that contains a workflow, diagram, or operational output requires a caption. The caption structure is fixed:

**[What this is] — [what engagement or scenario it represents] — [one operational fact it demonstrates]**

Example: "Lead intake workflow — built for a professional services firm with three qualification tiers — routes 100% of inbound leads without manual review."

Caption length: one sentence maximum. A caption that requires two sentences is trying to do explanatory work that the visual should handle.

**Captions are not optional.** An uncaptioned workflow screenshot is tech theater regardless of its content. The caption is what converts a visual from decoration to evidence.

### Acceptable Diagram Density

- Maximum 12 nodes in any workflow diagram shown on the homepage or About page.
- Maximum 20 nodes in a diagram on the Operational Records page, within a specific record.
- More than 20 nodes in a single viewport creates cognitive abandonment — the visitor cannot parse the diagram and stops trying.
- If a real workflow has 40+ nodes, show a segment of it with a caption noting the full scope. "Section of a 47-node lead qualification and routing system" is honest and more trust-building than an illegible full canvas.

### Acceptable Visual Abstraction Levels

**Level 0 — Real screenshot (highest trust):** An actual tool interface with real (or anonymized) data. No stylization.

**Level 1 — Annotated screenshot (high trust):** A real screenshot with callout annotations as described above. The underlying visual is real; the annotations add operational context.

**Level 2 — Simplified diagram (moderate trust):** A diagram that represents a real system using simplified visual language — boxes, arrows, labels. Acceptable when a full screenshot would be illegible. Required: the diagram must represent a real system, and the caption must identify it as a representation of a specific real workflow.

**Level 3 — Illustrative diagram (low trust, use sparingly):** A diagram that represents a general pattern rather than a specific system. Acceptable only for explaining a process concept (e.g., the Diagnose → Design → Implement → Optimize steps). Never acceptable as proof of work.

**Level 4 — Abstract visualization (no trust, forbidden):** Animated node diagrams with no labels, floating geometric elements representing "connection" or "flow," gradient network visualizations. These actively degrade trust by signaling that the company has no real systems to show.

### Image Sourcing Hierarchy

1. Real operational screenshots (first choice for all systems-related visuals)
2. Real working-context photos of the operator (first choice for About page)
3. Real environmental photography of operational contexts (audit materials, systems interfaces, documents — not stock photography of these things, but actual examples)
4. Simplified diagrams representing real systems (when screenshots are illegible)
5. Generic stock photography (last resort, only for non-operational context images — never for anything positioned as proof)

Stock photography of "professionals working" or "team collaboration" is forbidden at all positions on the site.

### Operational vs. Aesthetic Visual Test

Before any visual is included, apply this test:

1. Can a caption be written for this visual that names a specific operational fact?
2. Would removing this visual force a textual explanation that is longer and less clear?
3. If a skeptical visitor asked "what does this show," would the answer be specific?

If the answer to all three is yes: include the visual.  
If the answer to any is no: replace or remove.

## Part 4: Operator Image Doctrine

### Governing Principle

The operator image communicates a relationship before any word is read. Its primary function is to answer the visitor's implicit question: "Is this someone I trust to work inside my operation?" The image must deliver practitioner credibility — grounded, competent, and operational. It must not deliver brand impression.

### Acceptable Photo Types

- **Working context:** In the operator's actual work environment — at a desk with systems visible, reviewing documents, or in a context that communicates active engagement with operational work.
- **Environmental realism:** The background should be real — an actual workspace, not a staged studio backdrop or blank wall.
- **Low production:** Moderate production quality is correct. A professional photographer is appropriate; a studio shoot with lighting rigs and props is not. The image should look like a capable professional, not a brand asset.

### Unacceptable Photo Patterns

- Posed "arms crossed, looking at camera" executive portrait
- Studio white background (communicates brand, not operator)
- Looking at laptop in a coffee shop (communicates freelancer, not systems operator)
- Group photo or team imagery (there is no team — misleading)
- Casual selfie or low-quality snapshot (insufficient credibility signal)
- Any photo that required a wardrobe change from a typical work day (performative)
- Outdoor "thought leadership" walking or looking-into-distance photography (influencer register)

### Environmental Context

The photo should be taken in or near the actual operating environment. For an operator who builds systems: a desk with one or two monitors visible in the background, possibly showing a workflow canvas or development interface at low resolution (legible but not the focal point), is the ideal environmental signal.

What the environment should not contain: whiteboards covered in diagrams (performative), excessive tech hardware (tech theater), overly organized minimalist staging (aesthetic theater), or prominent brand elements (marketing register).

### Posture and Tone Constraints

- **Posture:** Neutral to slightly engaged. Not stiff. Not overly relaxed. The posture of someone who is comfortable in their work context.
- **Expression:** Calm, direct, neither smiling broadly nor appearing serious to the point of inaccessibility. The emotional register is "this person is competent and approachable" — not "this person is dynamic and exciting" and not "this person is formidable and serious."
- **Gaze:** Toward camera is standard and correct for a trust image. Looking away from camera creates ambiguity in a trust context.

### Lighting Constraints

- Natural light or natural-light equivalent is preferred.
- Dramatic directional lighting creates a high-contrast, high-production aesthetic that is not appropriate for the "practitioner" register.
- Flat lighting communicates a documentary or professional tone — appropriate.
- The image should not require significant post-processing. Over-processed images in a high-contrast or cinematic style signal aesthetic investment, not operational credibility.

### Wardrobe

- Consistent with how the operator works — if John typically works in a button-down or similar, that is correct.
- No suit or formal wear (executive register, not operator register).
- No branded gear (marketing register).
- No overly casual wear that would undermine a professional conversation.
- The implicit standard: what would be worn to a client's office for a working session?

### Emotional Signal Goals

The image should communicate, in order of priority:
1. Competence — this person knows what they're doing
2. Groundedness — this person is not performing or selling
3. Approachability — this person is accessible for a direct conversation
4. Practicality — this person works on real things

The image must not communicate:
- Excitement or enthusiasm (startup register)
- Authority or seniority hierarchy (executive register)
- Personal brand or thought leadership (influencer register)
- Formality beyond the work context (consultant theater)

## Part 5: Anti-Drift Enforcement Layer

### Purpose

This section functions as QA for positioning integrity. It is applied at the end of each copywriting session and before any section is handed to implementation. It is not a style guide — it is a regression detection system.

---

### Implementation Review Checklist

Apply to every completed section before it progresses to implementation.

**Proof integrity:**
- [ ] Does this section introduce any new proof not established elsewhere on the site?
- [ ] If yes: does that proof belong in this section, or on the Work/Operational Records page?
- [ ] Are all metrics anchored to a described operational situation?
- [ ] Does any metric appear in this section that also appears elsewhere? If yes: is this intentional repetition for reinforcement, or accidental duplication?

**Compression compliance:**
- [ ] Are all paragraphs within the 3-line maximum?
- [ ] Are all process step descriptions within the 2-sentence maximum?
- [ ] Does any sentence contain more than two clauses?
- [ ] Does any sentence contain more than two abstract nouns?
- [ ] Is the section-opening paragraph within the 2-line maximum?

**Density compliance:**
- [ ] What is this section's density level (high / medium / low)?
- [ ] What is the adjacent section's density level?
- [ ] Are two high-density sections adjacent? If yes: fix before implementation.
- [ ] Does this section introduce more than one new abstraction?
- [ ] Does every conceptual statement resolve into an operational example within 3 sentences?

**Scan compliance:**
- [ ] Does the section label communicate the section's point without reading the body?
- [ ] Does the first line of each paragraph front-load the point?
- [ ] Does the section work as a full read without contradicting the scan?

**Proof escalation compliance:**
- [ ] Is this page carrying its designated proof role (as defined in the Proof Density Doctrine)?
- [ ] Is this section carrying more proof than the page's role requires?

---

### "This Section Drifted" Indicators

A section has drifted if any of the following are true:

**Manifesto detection:**
- The section contains three or more consecutive sentences without a specific operational fact, example, or named output
- A sentence can be removed without losing any specific claim
- The section explains why IntraWeb's approach is correct rather than what it produces
- Any sentence begins with "We believe" or "Our philosophy"
- The section sounds like positioning language rather than operational description

**Proof inflation detection:**
- A metric appears without an operational situation that contextualizes it
- An outcome percentage is stated without a named before-state and after-state
- The section claims outcomes that are not documented in an Operational Record
- Numbers are rounded to suspiciously clean figures (50%, 100%, 10x) without a stated measurement basis

**AI-agency regression detection:**
- The words "AI," "automation," or "machine learning" appear in a section without naming a specific system, tool, or output
- Any sentence uses "AI" as a subject performing an action ("AI transforms your operations")
- The section leads with technology capability rather than operational problem
- Any use of: transform, streamline, leverage, unlock, supercharge, seamlessly, game-changing, next-level, future-proof, cutting-edge, intelligent, smart (as a modifier for a system)
- The section positions technology as the differentiator rather than the diagnostic-first, operational-understanding approach

**Cognitive overload detection:**
- The section contains more than 5 distinct pieces of information that must be held simultaneously
- Three or more new concepts are introduced before any are grounded in operational examples
- The section requires prior context from another section to be understood
- A visitor could not understand the section's core point from reading only the first line and the last line

**Trust erosion detection:**
- A claim is made without evidence and without direction to evidence elsewhere on the site
- The section uses hedging language that undermines prior claims ("may," "could," "in some cases," "depending on your situation" — without specifying what situation)
- A CTA asks for commitment before the visitor's state (as defined in the Conversion Flow Architecture) has been advanced to Imagination
- The section contradicts positioning established elsewhere on the site
- The section uses a different company descriptor than the master descriptor established in the navigation and identity language standardization

---

### Drift Severity Classification

**Severity 1 — Minor (fix before next session):**
A paragraph is one line over the limit. A section label is generic but not misleading. A sentence has three clauses.

**Severity 2 — Moderate (fix before implementation):**
A section introduces two abstractions without grounding either. An adjacent density violation. A metric floats without context. A sentence contains three abstract nouns.

**Severity 3 — Critical (fix before any further copywriting):**
AI-agency regression in a section that defines the company's positioning. Manifesto drift in the hero or above-fold content. Proof inflation in a metric that cannot be substantiated. A CTA placed before State 3 (Confidence) has been established.

Severity 3 violations require a full section rewrite, not an edit.

---

### Final Pre-Implementation Sign-Off Test

Before any section is handed off for Cursor implementation, it must pass all five questions:

1. **Specificity test:** Does every claim in this section resolve to a specific operational fact, named output, or documented outcome?
2. **Attribution test:** Could this section appear unchanged on any other operational consulting or automation agency website? If yes: it is not differentiated.
3. **Visitor state test:** Does this section advance the visitor from their entering state to their exiting state as defined in the Conversion Flow Architecture?
4. **Compression test:** Does any element of this section exceed its stated length constraint?
5. **Positioning test:** Does this section use the same company descriptor and positioning language as the rest of the site?

A section that fails any of these five tests does not proceed to implementation.
